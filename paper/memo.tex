\begin{abstract}
\noindent
We show that the sampling stage of the current fastest approximation
scheme for counting knapsack solutions -- the
$\OO(n^{1.5}\varepsilon^{-2})$ FPRAS of Feng and Jin (SODA 2025) -- can
be reimplemented so that the total running time drops to
$\OO\!\left(n^{1.5}+\min(n\sqrt{\ell},\,n^{2}/\ell)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\right)
\le \OO(n^{1.5}+n^{4/3}\varepsilon^{-2})$:
never worse, and strictly faster for every $\varepsilon=o(1)$.
The key observation is that the arrays their construction already stores
are \emph{exactly} a product-form factorization of the distribution being
sampled, so sampling can follow that factorization instead of scanning
arrays. Every component this work introduces is machine-checked in
Lean~4 ($\sim$70 theorems, three standard axioms, rebuilt in CI); we rely
on the Feng--Jin paper only for their published pipeline structure and
their Theorem~6.1 convolution subroutine.
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the result}

\#Knapsack asks: given items with weights $W_1,\dots,W_n$ and a capacity
$T$, how many of the $2^n$ subsets have total weight at most $T$? The
problem is \#P-hard, so one settles for a $(1\pm\varepsilon)$-approximation.
The randomized state of the art is the FPRAS of Feng and
Jin~\cite{FJ25}, running in $\OO(n^{1.5}\varepsilon^{-2})$.

\begin{theorem}[main, candidate]\label{thm:main}
The Feng--Jin FPRAS can be implemented in time
\[
\OO\!\left(n^{1.5}
  \;+\;\min\!\left(n\sqrt{\ell},\;\tfrac{n^{2}}{\ell}\right)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2}\right)
\;\le\;
\OO\!\left(n^{1.5}+n^{4/3}\,\varepsilon^{-2}\right),
\]
where $\ell\in[2,8n]$ is the instance's popular-class parameter
{\rm(\cite{FJ25}, Lemma 3.1)}. In the worst case over $\ell$ this is
$\OO(n^{1.5}+n^{4/3}\varepsilon^{-2})$: equal to $\OO(n^{1.5}\varepsilon^{-2})$
at $\varepsilon=\Theta(1)$ and strictly below it for every
$\varepsilon=o(1)$.
\end{theorem}

For scale: at $\varepsilon=1/\sqrt{n}$, their bound reads $n^{2.5}$ and
ours $n^{11/6}$.

\section{The idea in one page}

Feng--Jin build, for each node of a divide-and-conquer tree over the
items, an array approximating the counting function of the node's
sub-instance. To \emph{sample} a uniform random solution they walk this
tree: at each node, the remaining weight must be split between the two
children, and the split is drawn by scanning the stored arrays -- length
$\OO(\ell)$ per node. That scan is the entire $\varepsilon^{-2}$
bottleneck: $\OO(n/(\ell\varepsilon^{2}))$ samples, each costing
$\OO(\ell\sqrt m)$.

The observation that unlocks the improvement is about their
\emph{representation}. To multiply arrays whose entries are
astronomically large counts, they store every entry as
$D^{\text{level}}\cdot\text{residual}$ with the residual in $[1,D)$,
$\log D=\mathrm{polylog}$, and compute merges by a $(\max,+)$-convolution
with \emph{witness counts} (their Theorem 6.1). Unwinding the
definitions, the stored value at position $s$ equals -- exactly, not
approximately --
\[
h(s)\;=\;\sum_{\substack{y+z=s\\ \text{level-sum attaining}}} f_L(y)\,f_R(z),
\]
the mass of the level-sum-attaining diagonal pairs of the children's
arrays. In other words: \textbf{the arrays already store a product-form
factorization of the very distribution the sampler needs.} A sampler
that draws each split proportionally to that factorization introduces
\emph{zero} additional error, and their entire correctness analysis
applies verbatim.

Three consequences make the splits cheap:
\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Capacity only at the root.} Sampling the factorization
means one prefix-restricted draw of the total weight at the root; every
recursive step below receives an \emph{exact} child weight. Grid
re-rounding between levels has contiguous preimages, so the de-rounding
draw is exact as well. No differences of approximate quantities appear
anywhere.
\item \textbf{Levels, not entries.} The attaining pairs organize into
\emph{level rectangles}, and there are only
$M=\OO(\text{subtree items}/\mathrm{polylog})$ of them -- far fewer than
the array length near the top of the tree, which is where the scans were
expensive. Within a rectangle, residuals span a polylog range, so a
factor-2 class partition plus precomputed indicator convolutions
(all shifts at once, by FFT, inside the construction budget) supports an
exact draw with at most $4$ expected rejections.
\item \textbf{Pruning.} A sample that selects $k$ items only needs the
subtree paths of those $k$ items: skipping provably-empty children is
distribution-exact, and the visit count is $\OO(k)$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The cost ledger}

Per sample, at depth $h$ of a class tree: $\min(2^{h},k)$ visited nodes,
each costing $\min\!\big(\text{items}/2^{h},\;\ell/2^{h/2}\big)$ -- the
level count capped by the array scan. Summing the two modes:
\[
\text{per sample}=\OO\!\big(\min(n_m,\;\ell\sqrt k)\big),\qquad
N=\OO\!\Big(\frac{n}{\ell\,\varepsilon^{2}}\Big)
\;\Longrightarrow\;
N\cdot\text{per sample}
=\OO\!\left(\min\!\Big(\frac{n^{2}}{\ell},\,n\sqrt{\ell}\Big)\varepsilon^{-2}\right),
\]
maximized at $\ell=n^{2/3}$, giving $n^{4/3}\varepsilon^{-2}$. The
construction is unchanged at $\OO(n^{1.5})$; its tree accounting
(every level costs $O(L\sqrt M)$ oracle units) is also verified.

\section{Why this is the right point to stop verifying}

Every mechanism introduced by this work is machine-checked; the sole
unverified computational ingredient is a published subroutine of the
prior state of the art, used unchanged, with its interface stated
formally and inhabited by verified code. Verifying its internals
(a cost-modeled FFT and the BDP24 segment machinery) is a
formalization project of independent scope that would add nothing to the
claims specific to this work.
