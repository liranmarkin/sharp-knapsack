\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The fastest known approximation scheme for counting knapsack solutions
is the $\OO(n^{1.5}\varepsilon^{-2})$ FPRAS of Feng and Jin (SODA 2025).
We observe that the arrays their construction stores are, exactly, a
product-form factorization of the distribution their sampler needs, and
that sampling along this factorization -- instead of scanning arrays --
drops the total running time to
$\OO(n^{1.5}+\min(n\sqrt{\ell},n^{2}/\ell)\cdot\varepsilon^{-2})
\le\OO(n^{1.5}+n^{4/3}\varepsilon^{-2})$:
never worse, and strictly faster for every
$\varepsilon=o(1)$.\footnote{A Lean~4 development accompanying this memo
machine-checks the components introduced here; see the repository.}
\end{abstract}

\section{The problem and the result}

\#Knapsack asks: given item weights $W_1,\dots,W_n$ and a capacity $T$,
how many of the $2^{n}$ subsets have total weight at most $T$? The
problem is \#P-hard, so one settles for a randomized
$(1\pm\varepsilon)$-approximation (an FPRAS). After two decades of
progress (Dyer's $\OO(n^{2.5}\varepsilon^{-2})$~\cite{Dyer03} through
several deterministic and randomized improvements), the record is the
$\OO(n^{1.5}\varepsilon^{-2})$ algorithm of Feng and Jin~\cite{FJ25}.

\begin{theorem}\label{thm:main}
The Feng--Jin FPRAS can be implemented in time
\[
\OO\!\left(n^{1.5}
  \;+\;\min\!\left(n\sqrt{\ell},\;\tfrac{n^{2}}{\ell}\right)\cdot
  \varepsilon^{-2}\right)
\;\le\;
\OO\!\left(n^{1.5}+n^{4/3}\,\varepsilon^{-2}\right),
\]
where $\ell\in[2,8n]$ is the instance's popular weight-class parameter
{\rm(\cite{FJ25}, Lemma~3.1)}.
\end{theorem}

At $\varepsilon=\Theta(1)$ the two bounds coincide at $n^{1.5}$; the
moment $\varepsilon$ shrinks, the $\varepsilon^{-2}$ term dominates and
the coefficient drops from $n^{1.5}$ to at most $n^{4/3}$. For scale:
at $\varepsilon=1/\sqrt n$, their bound reads $n^{2.5}$, ours
$n^{11/6}$.

\section{Where their time goes}

Feng--Jin follow Dyer's recipe: count a rounded, easier instance
exactly-ish, then correct the count by estimating, via random sampling,
the fraction of rounded solutions that are true solutions. Both halves
run on the same data structure: a divide-and-conquer tree over the
items, where each node stores an array approximating the counting
function of its sub-instance ($\text{array}[s]\approx$ number of
sub-solutions of rounded weight $s$).

Construction of all arrays costs $\OO(n^{1.5})$. The expensive half is
\emph{sampling}: drawing one uniform random rounded solution means
walking the tree from the root, and at every node splitting the
remaining weight between the two children with probability proportional
to a product of the children's array entries. Their sampler draws this
split by scanning the arrays -- length $\OO(\ell)$ at the top of a class
tree -- so each of the $N=\OO(n/(\ell\varepsilon^{2}))$ samples costs
$\OO(\ell\sqrt m)$, and the sampling stage totals
$\OO(n^{1.5}\varepsilon^{-2})$. That scan is the entire
$\varepsilon^{-2}$ bottleneck.

\section{The observation}

The improvement comes from looking closely at the \emph{representation}
Feng--Jin already use. Array entries are counts as large as $2^{n}$, so
to multiply arrays in subquadratic time they store every entry in the
form $D^{\text{level}}\cdot\text{residual}$ with the residual in
$[1,D)$, $\log D=\mathrm{polylog}$, and merge two children with a
bounded $(\max,+)$-convolution that also returns \emph{witness counts}
(their Theorem~6.1): for each output position, the total mass of the
level-sum-\emph{maximal} pairs.

Unwinding their definitions, the merged value stored at position $s$
satisfies, exactly,
\[
h(s)\;=\;\sum_{\substack{y+z=s\\ \text{level-sum attaining}}}
  f_{L}(y)\,f_{R}(z),
\]
because on every witness pair the level scaling cancels:
$D^{C}\cdot u(y)v(z)=f_{L}(y)f_{R}(z)$. This is an identity, not an
approximation. In words:

\begin{quote}
\emph{The arrays already store a product-form factorization of the
distribution the sampler needs. The witness machinery was built to
\emph{compute} the arrays fast; as an unused by-product, it makes them
\emph{samplable} fast.}
\end{quote}

A sampler that draws each split proportionally to this stored
factorization therefore introduces \emph{no additional error at all} --
the tree of arrays defines a distribution, and we sample exactly it, so
the Feng--Jin correctness analysis (which compares that distribution to
the uniform one) applies verbatim.

\section{The sampler}

One sample proceeds as follows.

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
\item \textbf{Root draw.} Draw the total rounded weight
$s\propto\hat f_{\mathrm{root}}(s)$ over $s\le T$, by one binary search
in a precomputed prefix table. This is the \emph{only} place the
capacity constraint appears.
\item \textbf{De-rounding.} Positions are re-rounded to coarser grids up
the tree. Rounding is a monotone floor, so each coarse position's
preimage is a contiguous interval of the finer grid; drawing the fine
position within it is another exact interval draw. Consequently every
recursive step below receives an \emph{exact} child weight -- no
differences of approximate quantities ever arise.
\item \textbf{Split draw.} At a node with exact position $s$, draw an
attaining pair $(y,\,s-y)$ with probability $\propto u(y)v(s-y)$. The
attaining pairs organize into \emph{level rectangles} -- pairs of
intervals with a fixed level sum -- and there are only
$M=\OO(\text{subtree items}/\mathrm{polylog})$ of them, far fewer than
the array length near the root, which is exactly where the old scans
were expensive. Within a rectangle the residuals span a polylog range,
so a factor-2 bucketing of values plus precomputed indicator
convolutions (all shifts at once, one FFT per node, inside the
construction budget) supports an exact draw with at most four expected
rejections.
\item \textbf{Pruning.} If a child's drawn weight is $0$ and its only
weight-$0$ subset is the empty set, emit $\varnothing$ without
descending. This is distribution-exact, and it means a sample that
contains $k$ items visits only the $\OO(k)$ tree paths of those items.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Why this is $n^{4/3}$}

Per sample, at depth $h$ of a weight-class tree, the pruned walk visits
$\min(2^{h},k)$ nodes, and each split draw costs
$\min(\text{items}/2^{h},\;\ell/2^{h/2})$ -- the rectangle count capped
by the plain scan (the new method is never slower than the old one).
Summing over depths,
\[
\text{per sample}\;=\;\OO\!\big(\min(n_m,\;\ell\sqrt{k})\big),
\qquad k=\OO(\ell),
\]
and multiplying by the sample count $N=\OO(n/(\ell\varepsilon^{2}))$:
\[
N\cdot\text{per sample}
\;=\;\OO\!\left(\min\!\Big(\tfrac{n^{2}}{\ell},\;n\sqrt{\ell}\Big)\,
\varepsilon^{-2}\right).
\]
The two branches cross at $\ell=n^{2/3}$, where the value is
$n^{4/3}\varepsilon^{-2}$ -- the worst case of
Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. At the extremes ($\ell=\mathrm{polylog}$ or
$\ell=\Theta(n)$) the bound improves further, to
$\OO(n^{1.5}+n\,\varepsilon^{-2})$.

\section{The refinement: honest rounding}

The $n^{4/3}$ kink -- and all the amortization machinery one can build
to soften it -- turns out to be an artifact of one coupling: the grid
step is tied to $T/\ell^2$, which makes the rounded count overshoot by
$1/p = \OO(n/\ell)$ and forces $N = \OO(n/(\ell\varepsilon^2))$
samples. The coupling is not forced by correctness, only by the query
cost: the original sampler \emph{reads} arrays, so finer grids explode
its sampling phase. The pruned scan of Section~4 never reads positions
-- it enumerates witness levels -- so its per-sample cost is
independent of the grid. That frees the grid entirely:

\begin{quote}
\emph{Make the grid fine enough that the rounded count is honest
($p = \Theta(1/\mathrm{polylog})$), and pay for it only in array
length, which the pruned scan never reads.}
\end{quote}

With grid step $\sim T/(\ell n)$ the total round-down error stays
below one band width $T/\ell$, so the overshoot is the band mass --
polylogarithmic by the same Lemma~3.3 that powers their sample bound
-- and $N = \OO(\varepsilon^{-2})$. Construction is per-merge
proportional to length times $\sqrt{\text{levels}}$, summing to
$\OO(n^{1.5})$ at full boost, independent of $\ell$. The total is
\[
\OO\!\left(n^{1.5} + n\,\varepsilon^{-2}\right)
\qquad\text{uniformly in $\varepsilon$ and $\ell$:}
\]
construction plus output-optimal sampling, with no residual
$\varepsilon$-term at all. This subsumes the amortized-caching
refinements described in earlier versions of this memo.

\section{Discussion}

Two remarks. First, the bound is \emph{instance-adaptive}: $\ell$ is
computable in $\OO(n)$ time, so the algorithm knows its own speed in
advance, and only a narrow band of instances around
$\ell\approx n^{2/3}$ realizes the worst case. Second, the remaining
bottleneck is transparent: the per-draw rectangle enumeration. Removing
it -- sampling a rectangle in polylog time -- would give
$\OO(n^{1.5}+n\,\varepsilon^{-2})$ uniformly, and any further progress
below $n^{1.5}$ is blocked by the bounded monotone
$(\max,+)$-convolution frontier, matching the open problems stated
in~\cite{FJ25}.
